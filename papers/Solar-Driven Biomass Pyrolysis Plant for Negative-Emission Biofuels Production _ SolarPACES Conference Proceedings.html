SolarPACES Conference Proceedings, 3. https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v3i.2438 Abstract As part of the Horizon Europe Project PYSOLO, this study focuses on the integration of a 10 MWTH fast pyrolysis plant with a solar tower system equipped with a rotary kiln particle receiver. Heated particles act as a particle heat carrier (PHC) for the endothermic pyrolysis process, enhancing flexibility and allowing continuous operation compared to directly heated solar reactors. The system's design, techno-economic analysis and storage capacity optimization are performed considering two operation modes, solar-only and hybrid, in which biochar is combusted in absence of solar energy. Results indicate that, due to the rotary kiln flow regime constraints, its tilt angle has to be limited in a range between 0.5-2° to guarantee particles being in rolling mode, thus penalizing the achievable solar field optical efficiency. On the other hand solar-based pyrolysis can achieve carbon efficiencies close to 0.9 and, thanks to the carbon stored in biochar, net negative emissions equal to -27.05 and -19.45 kgCO2/GJOIL respectively in solar-only and hybrid mode. Moreover, as the pyrolysis section has the most relevant cost share, the system optimization pushes to maximise the pyrolysis plant operating hours (and thus the bio-oil production) to reduce the CAPEX impact on the Minimum Fuel Selling Price (MFSP). For this reason hybrid mode results in being the most convenient (i.e: MFSP of 25.71 vs 21.36 €/GJOIL). 1. Introduction According to the European Union (EU) the fraction of renewables in the EU energy mix is expected to grow up to at least 42.5% by 2030 [1], an ambitious threshold that has driven research and industry efforts in developing new ways of exploiting renewable energy sources. Nowadays bioenergy provides 12.6% of the overall energy consumption [2] and can be converted into higher density energy vectors either by a thermochemical or biochemical process. Thermochemical conversion benefits from faster chemical reactions, suitability for a wider variety of biomass feedstock, higher energy efficiency and advantages of scalability compared to biochemical processes. Moreover, thermochemical conversion is not subject to seasonal and environmental limitations as biochemical and allows year-long operation with better control of operating conditions [3]. Based on the amount of supplied oxygen, a thermochemical process can be classified as pyrolysis, combustion or gasification. Pyrolysis in particular has gathered great interest due to its potential of converting any type of solid biomass into bio-oil, whose most promising application is co-processing in Fluid Catalytic Crackers (FCC) in existing fossil refineries [4], and/or biochar, a way to concentrate stable carbon in large amounts and use it in agriculture as soil amendment, as heat, as material in a variety of applications or as a carbon sequestration medium [5]. The pyrolysis process is endothermic and requires an external energy source, such as direct combustion of part of the feedstock/products or electric heating, to heat up the feedstock and break down the molecular structure of the carbonaceous material [6], [7], [8], [9]. The limit of conventional pyrolysis processes sustained by combustion is the loss of part of the biogenic carbon as CO2, increasing the environmental impact and losing a valuable asset, thus making the process more energy intensive and costly. In the literature, the adoption of Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) as energy source to overcome those limits has been gaining attention, considering different feedstocks, types of concentrators, reactors and process configurations [6], [7], [8], [9]. As part of the Horizon Europe Project PYSOLO [10], this analysis focuses on the integration of the pyrolysis unit with a solar tower system equipped with a rotary kiln particle receiver: the particles heated in the tower are directly used as heat carrier (particle heat carrier, PHC) in the pyrolyzer to provide the required thermal power for the endothermic pyrolysis process. Compared to directly irradiated biomass reactors, the decoupling between solar receiver and pyrolizer enhances flexibility and allows continuous operation with possible thermal energy storage integration. On the other hand, the adoption of a particle receiver in place of a commercial molten salts solar tower is meant to avoid the molten salts high corrosiveness, the high solidification temperature that makes start-up and shut-down problematic causing high parasitic losses, and the need of a high temperature heat transfer surface thanks to the direct contact between PHC and biomass. In the following study, the preliminary design and the techno-economic analysis of a 10 MWTH fast pyrolysis plant for bio-oil and biochar production integrated with a rotary kiln particle solar tower system are performed comparing different plant configurations (solar-only and hybrid). For each plant configuration, the storage capacity and the solar multiple (SM) are optimized in order to minimize the minimum fuel selling price (MFSP), which is obtained following a net present value (NPV) analysis. Being biomass made of biogenic carbon and thus being carbon neutral, focus is given to the potential negative CO2 emissions of both plant configurations, which, by storing or using as soil amendment the biochar produced, can act as a carbon sequestrator plant. 2. System Modelling This section introduces the main modelling assumptions of the pyrolysis plant, of the rotary kiln solar receiver and of the economic and profitability analysis. 2.1 Pyrolysis Plant The configuration of the fast pyrolysis section is taken from Jones et al. [7], where a biorefinery converting woody biomass into high-value liquid fuel was modelled. In a previous study [11], the model has been replicated and slightly modified to account for the solar integration adopting Aspen Plus V14 [12] to compute mass and energy balances. The plant is sized to convert a biomass input of 50 dry t/d (10 MWTH on a lower heating value basis) and is divided into 5 main sections: Biomass Pretreatment: for an efficient conversion, biomass is dried till reaching a moisture content of 10% by a stream of hot flue gases and is then grinded to 2 mm particles to ease the pyrolysis reaction. The temperature of the hot PHC is 609°C and the flowrate is controlled to maintain the reactor outlet temperature equal to 434°C. The fluidized bed pyrolyzer is modeled as a black box with fixed biomass input, replicating the yield and product composition reported by Jones et al. [7] and considering the PHC as chemically inert. Solid Removal: the products enter the cyclone filter, which separates volatile products from solids entrained from the reactor at high temperatures. Bio-Oil Recovery: quenching columns condense pyrolysis vapours and collect bio-oil. Non-condensable gases generate pyrogas (mainly CO, CO2, CH4), mostly used as a fluidizing medium with the remainder combusted to generate heat for the drying process. Bio-oil is filtered to remove solid particles along with a small portion of oil, generating "sludge." Solid Separator: the PHC is separated from the biochar and sent to the cold solid Thermal Energy Storage (TES) after a make-up to compensate for PHC losses. Two plant configurations are analysed: solar-only (plant works only when solar energy is available) and hybrid (plant always works, compensating absence of solar energy by burning sludge and a fraction of the produced biochar in a combustor to heat the PHC). KPIs: pyrolysis plant energy conversion efficiency η(pyro-plant) = ratio of products' chemical energy (bio-oil, biochar, sludge) to primary energy input (biomass + auxiliary electricity converted at ηel,ref = 50% + PHC thermal power). Solar optical-to-thermal efficiency η(sol-th) covers solar field + receiver efficiency. Carbon efficiency εc = ratio of carbon mass fractions of products to biomass carbon. Emission-to-oil ratio (ETO) = yearly CO2 emissions / bio-oil production; net negative emission-to-oil ratio (ETOnet) treats biogenic CO2 as climate-neutral and credits carbon stored in biochar. Solarizing rotary kilns presents challenges: effective solar radiation penetration for homogeneous temperature distribution (especially at significant length); limiting re-irradiation losses from the kiln aperture; cooling/cleaning of the kiln window aperture and secondary mirror; and selecting an optimal tilt angle trading off solar field efficiency against process parameters (bed motion, particle mixing, residence time, etc.) [13], [14]. Particle flow regime depends on the Froude number (ratio of centripetal to gravitational acceleration, function of kiln rotational speed and diameter). To operate in rolling mode (maximizing particle mixing) the Froude number must range between 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻². Two flow regions: the shearing "active layer" near the free surface, and the passive/plug-flow region at the bottom (zero shear rate) [16], [17]. Other design parameters: Fill Ratio (FR) — fraction of rotary kiln cross-section occupied by particles, typically 0.1–0.25 [13]; kiln residence time τres — depends on kiln tilt angle, angular speed, and aspect ratio (L/D), computed here via the Lee and Lin [18] correlation; dam height — maintains particle bed height along the kiln axis, set equal to bed height in this study [13]. Design procedure: combining the Froude number, fill ratio, tilt angle, PHC bulk density and PHC flowrate constraints yields the kiln diameter. A fill ratio of 0.1 was chosen to maximize kiln diameter (helping solar field optical efficiency). The design Froude number is set so its minimum boundary is reached at 20% partial load, giving a minimum Froude boundary of 2.5×10⁻³. Results show that increasing tilt angle (beneficial for solar field design) reduces the allowed aperture size and increases the required heat flux to potentially unfeasible values; for the studied case, tilt angles need to stay below 4–5° (fluxes below 2–3 MW/m²), with rotational speed increased to compensate and maintain constant Froude number. Solar field design: Seville (37.39°N, 5.99°W) selected as plant site; design DNI of 900 W/m² assumed at solar noon on June 21. Designed with SolarPILOT [19] using a flat-plate receiver, square aperture equal to kiln aperture diameter; a correction coefficient of 0.94 applied to optical efficiency (derived by comparing SolarPILOT square-aperture export to SolTrace [20] circular-aperture results, since SolarPILOT cannot simulate circular apertures directly). Tower height varied 60–120 m to maximize power absorbed by the PHC at design conditions; particle-lift consumption included in auxiliaries. Conclusion - Solar-based pyrolysis can achieve over 90% carbon efficiency: about 70% of inlet biogenic carbon retained in bio-oil, about 20% in biochar. Net negative emissions of -27.05 (solar-only) and -19.45 (hybrid) kgCO2/GJ_OIL. - The kiln "rolling mode" flow-regime constraint (Froude number range) limits feasible tilt angles to 0.5–2°, generating challenging solar fluxes on the kiln aperture. Possible mitigations: spiral lifters to increase PHC residence time [28]; increasing PHC mass flowrate at constant thermal power (reducing ΔT); PHCs with lower bulk density/heat capacity; or two kilns in parallel to halve mass flowrate. - Solar-only mode has better environmental indices (ETO 13.09 vs 21.92 kgCO2/GJ_OIL) by avoiding biochar combustion, but lower pyrolysis plant equivalent hours (7053 vs 8760 h) increase payback time and reduce NPV, favoring the economics of hybrid mode (lowest MFSP: 25.71 vs 21.36 €/GJ_OIL).
